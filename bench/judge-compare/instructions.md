# How to judge a slide build the way Mike does

One picture shows two slides: the ORIGINAL from a real consulting deck on the LEFT, and a BUILD an AI made from a text brief on the RIGHT. Say whether Mike, the owner, would accept the build next to the original.

Mike's goal is mimicry: "bare minimum is you should be able to mimic". He wants no pixel copy and audits no cells. He asks: does the build give the same overall impression, as a complete, professionally made slide? When he rejects builds, he says: the originals "look more professional", the builds "look AI and not professional", "sometimes there are white blank gaps", and of one small chart, it "looks ugly somehow".

## How to look

1. Look at both halves, then zoom into each region of the build (title, every chart, table or box, footer) and compare it with the same region of the original. If you crop or save any image, save it only in your own /tmp folder named in your prompt. Zoom to confirm, not to hunt for tiny faults.
2. Check how every exhibit is drawn, small side charts included, not only whether it is there.
3. Then decide on the whole slide. Only faults that change the overall impression make a build NOT OK.

## Slide-level faults (these make a build NOT OK)

A fault is slide-level when it is in the title or the main visual, when it repeats across the slide, when it covers a large area (roughly a quarter of the slide or a whole column), when several different faults appear together, or when one exhibit is drawn clumsily (below).

**Design dropped and not replaced.** The original has a clear design treatment (a dark or coloured background, shaded panels, boxes, cards, a filled header band), and the build drops it and puts nothing designed in its place, leaving plain default text on white. A bare page of paragraphs fails even with every word there.

**White blank gaps.** A large empty area where the original has content: a blank band or bottom third, a half-empty column, charts floating in white.

**Careless text across the slide.** Body text much smaller than the original's everywhere; bullets flattened into run-on sentences or hierarchy lost; repeated glitches (doubled bullet marks, mixed font sizes); the title or a header band clipped or jammed against an edge.

**Charts stripped or broken.** The main chart loses its parts (axis, connector lines, total labels, a side panel of figures, subtitles, legend), its colours become flat default greys, or its labels overlap so badly that most cannot be read.

**Structure lost.** The original's building blocks (takeaway boxes, numbered section headers, connecting arrows, row labels) are dropped or replaced by plain text, or content is poured into a generic grid.

**One exhibit drawn clumsily.** Even one small chart is enough. It fails when, beside the original's neat drawing, it looks awkward at a glance: a segment shrunk to a thin sliver with its label squeezed inside it, a shape much chunkier than the original's, labels floating loose instead of tied to what they name, or the device the original used to keep it readable (a break mark, leader lines, callouts) dropped with nothing as neat in its place. A complete, tidy rest of the slide does not save it.

**Main visual reduced, not just restyled.** For a map or matrix with items placed on it, a plainer drawing (fewer shades, flat cells, some item names moved to a legend beside it) is only a remark when it keeps its frame (axis lines or arrows, axis titles, Low/Medium/High labels), every numbered item in its position, and the highlight around the same area. It is NOT OK ("could be better") when the redraw also loses part of that: items dropped, merged or moved, the highlight around the wrong area, or no axis lines, so the grid stands as loose grey blocks. For a timeline or flow, the same test: every item and step kept in place.

## Local flaws (tolerate these when the rest is complete and well made)

- One empty cell or one short gap in an otherwise full layout.
- One text block slightly past its box, or text tight to a cell edge.
- A few crowded or slightly misplaced data labels in a chart that keeps all its parts.
- Wrong values, wrong bar lengths, or some rows without their bars. Mike judges the look, not the data.
- Different colours or background, replaced by an equally designed treatment of its own (numbered cards, shaded boxes).
- Restyling the same structure: filled shapes for outlined ones, a shaded table for grid lines, arrows for plain bars, swapped series colours.
- A different chart form that is equally rich and equally neat (for example a clean pie for a stacked bar, every part labelled clearly).
- A plainer but complete main visual, as described above: mention it in your reason.
- Loose placement or spacing, missing logos, a different footer.

## Where the line is

OK: an imperfect but professional mimic. Same message and main parts, space used as fully, text readable, every exhibit drawn neatly, and a designed look (the original's or an equally good one).

NOT OK: at least one slide-level fault, so that next to the original the build looks plainer, emptier, broken, generic, or has one chart that looks ugly. A different style does not sink a polished, complete build.

If unsure, ask whether Mike would notice the fault within seconds at normal size. If not, it is local. The one exception is a clumsy exhibit: he notices those.

## How to answer

Answer in exactly two lines.

Line 1: `OK` or `NOT OK`
Line 2: one short reason naming the main thing you saw (for example: "design dropped, left as plain paragraphs on white" or "complete, polished mimic; map plainer than the original but all risks and axes kept").
